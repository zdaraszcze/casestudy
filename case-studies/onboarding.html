Case study, private banking

Private-bank client onboarding: from 55 days to two weeks for complex cases

At a Swiss private bank, complex onboarding cases took up to 55 days, and most of that time was spent re-entering data and reworking due diligence files. I led research with 38 people in four roles, then designed an onboarding flow where advisors enter data once and the system checks it straight away.

Role
Product designer, research lead
Engagement
10 weeks, Q1 to Q2 2025
Research
38 participants, 120 survey responses
Status
MVP released

Problem

Onboarding spanned 9 phases, 148 steps, and more than 20 disconnected systems. Only about 1 in 5 due diligence files passed review the first time, and every return restarted a chain of 14 approvals.

What I did

I ran contextual inquiry, a diary study, and interviews with advisors, specialists, due diligence officers, and clients. I then designed eight improvements, one for each phase in scope, and a clickable prototype that covers every role from intake to signing.

Outcome

The MVP has been released, and the people using it like it. The goal is to complete complex cases in 14 days or less, down from 35 to 55 days, and to raise first-time-right due diligence from about 20% to 70%.

01

The situation

At JPB Global Wealth Management, three teams share client onboarding. Client Advisors own the relationship, Onboarding Specialists prepare the formal documents, and Due Diligence Officers decide on risk. A standard case took 3 to 5 days. Most new high-net-worth relationships need enhanced due diligence, and those cases took 35 to 55 days.

The process had never been designed end to end. It grew one regulation and one system at a time, and people bridged the gaps by hand: copying the same address into three to five systems, running the PEP check in a separate Excel file, merging PDFs in Acrobat, and requesting documents by email. The platform didn't check anything, so quality depended on how careful each person was. Meanwhile, clients heard nothing for weeks, then received requests for documents they had already sent.

Constraints

  • Regulated decisions stay with people. Any use of AI in risk classification or in place of the four-eye control needs Compliance and Legal approval, and may have to be reported to the regulator.
  • Core Banking was outside the project's scope. The 3-day account block comes from an overnight archive batch, and changing it needs a separate programme.
  • Out of scope for this release: lead generation, portfolio management after onboarding, and a native mobile app.
02

Research and findings

I combined watching people work with asking them about it. Contextual inquiry showed what advisors and specialists actually did during live cases, while the diary study and interviews explained why. A System Usability Scale survey with 120 responses gave a baseline score for the current tools.

MethodParticipantsWhat it gave us
Contextual inquiry8Shadowing advisors and specialists for 3 to 4 hours during live cases, noting every switch between systems
Diary study12 advisorsOver 10 working days, advisors logged the systems they used, time per task, and moments of frustration
In-depth interviews1860 to 90 minutes across all four roles, including high-net-worth clients
Usability survey120System Usability Scale baseline for current tools

  1. Data captured in one system never reached the others

    The onboarding platform did not pass data to the advisor portal or the document generator, so generated documents came out with blank or wrong fields. Advisors typed the same client data into three to five systems per case.

    “I fill in the same client address in the onboarding form, then again in the document tool, then again when we need to generate a letter.” Client Advisor

  2. The due diligence problem started in the front office

    The bank treated due diligence as a back-office bottleneck. Watching officers work showed that most files were returned because the source-of-wealth narrative was too thin, and that officers rarely disagreed with the advisor about the risk itself. An officer could often tell within seconds that a file would come back, but still had to process it formally first. That meant the fix had to happen earlier, while the advisor writes the case.

    “The advisor submits what they think is enough. I can see within 30 seconds it will need to come back. But I have to process it formally before I can return it.” Due Diligence Officer

  3. Specialists spent most of their time handling files

    Formalities took 28 manual steps spread across the platform, document generation by email, Acrobat, a QR code tool, and a shared drive with no audit trail. Specialists estimated that about 60% of their time in this phase was spent moving and preparing files.

    “I spend more time creating folder structures and flattening PDFs than I do checking whether a case is actually complete.” Client Onboarding Specialist

  4. Clients lost goodwill at the finish line

    Clients who had verified their identity with a paper passport or ID card could not sign electronically without a scheduled video call, and accounts stayed blocked for up to 3 days after signing. The bank considered the case finished at signing, but the client still couldn't use the account.

    “Three weeks later I was still receiving requests for documents I had already provided. By the time it opened, I had already started conversations with another bank.” High-net-worth client

03

Key decisions

1Check due diligence quality while the advisor writes the case

As the advisor writes the source-of-wealth narrative and uploads documents, a built-in assistant checks them against the source-of-wealth checklist and names what is missing, such as an approximate amount or the year the funds were received. It is guidance only: the officer still makes every risk decision.

Set aside
A pre-screening step before review. It would lengthen a chain that already has 14 approvals, and the rework would just move to that step rather than go away.
Cost
Using generative AI anywhere in the risk process needs Compliance and Legal sign-off and model governance. The first version runs the same checks as fixed rules, so the screen can ship even if AI approval takes longer.

2One case record that every system reads from

The system reads the passport copy on upload and fills in the case, and the advisor confirms the details against the copy. The data then flows back to the advisor portal and the document generator. The PEP check runs inside the platform and saves its results to the case, so the advisor works the whole case in one place.

Set aside
Improving each system's screens separately. Better forms in five systems would still mean typing the same address five times.
Cost
It depends on two APIs that do not exist yet: writeback to the advisor portal and access to the PEP screening tool. Both are owned by other teams.

3Formalities as one submission, with people reviewing exceptions

The system generates documents from the approved case, with the client classification, booking centre, and e-signature eligibility already filled in. A pre-check lists only what needs attention, and the specialist signs off the package before anything is sent.

Set aside
Dropping the four-eye control in the first release. The second reviewer stays and works from the exception list. The control could be replaced later, but only with Compliance approval.
Cost
This removes about 60% of what specialists do today, so the role needs a change plan agreed with HR and Operations before rollout.

4Show every person where the case stands

Advisors, specialists, officers, and the client see the same status: the current phase, who owns it, and when it should be done. Clients verify their identity in the bank's app by scanning the passport chip and taking a selfie, so they can sign without scheduling a call.

Set aside
Email updates at key milestones, which clients were already getting. Between emails they heard nothing, and that silence was their main complaint.
Cost
The status can show the 3-day account block but cannot remove it, because that needs the Core Banking programme. Legal must also confirm which clients can identify themselves online.

5A clearance score broken down by factor

The officer sees a clearance score from 0 to 100, where higher means fewer risk indicators. It is broken down into the factors behind it: identity, screening, source of wealth, ownership, adverse media, and cross-border booking. Each factor links to its evidence.

Set aside
A single AI risk rating, which my first concept showed as “AI risk: low, 87/100”. It read as a verdict that an auditor could not trace, and it effectively let the model make the decision.
Cost
Officers may still anchor on the number. Showing the factors and their evidence next to it, and requiring the officer's signature on every decision, reduces that risk but does not remove it.

The design

The design includes eight improvements, one for each phase in scope. Three of them needed regulatory or legal approval before they could be built.

These six screens from the clickable prototype show the points in the journey where time used to be lost. Hover over or select a note to see where it points on the screen.

Client Advisor, intake

Source of wealth

  1. 1
    Gaps found at intake. The assistant checks the narrative and evidence against the source-of-wealth checklist while the advisor works, and says how many items are missing.
  2. 2
    Each gap says what to add. “Add an approximate amount” and “Add the year the funds were received” are specific enough to fix in one pass.
  3. 3
    Documents checked on upload. The system checks each upload against the requested document type and compares names and dates with the case.
  4. 4
    Guidance only. The assistant does not decide. Due Diligence makes the assessment, and the advisor can still submit with an open gap.
Client Advisor, intake

Identity

  1. 1
    Read from the passport copy. Name, date of birth, nationality, and passport details are taken from the upload and fill in the case automatically.
  2. 2
    The advisor confirms. A checkbox records that the advisor checked the extracted details against the copy before the case moves on.
  3. 3
    No scheduled call. The client verifies in the bank’s app by scanning the passport chip and taking a selfie, which replaces the video identification call.
Client Advisor

Case progress

  1. 1
    Next step and its owner. The case page leads with what happens next and who it is waiting for.
  2. 2
    One timeline for every role. Each phase shows who owns it and when it should be done, from intake to account access.
  3. 3
    Expected access date. The page shows when the client can expect account access after the overnight verification run. Removing the delay needs the Core Banking programme.
Due Diligence Officer

Officer review

  1. 1
    Clearance score by factor. The score runs from 0 to 100, where higher means fewer risk indicators, and is broken into the factors behind it.
  2. 2
    Non-blocking findings stay visible. A settled dispute lowers the score slightly and stays on screen for the officer to judge.
  3. 3
    Where the narrative came from. The source-of-wealth text shows how it was produced: drafted from documents, reviewed by the advisor, and confirmed by the client.
  4. 4
    The approval chain in view. All 14 sign-offs are listed, with the officer’s position in the chain.
  5. 5
    Precise follow-up. “Request information” asks for the specific missing item instead of returning the whole case.
Onboarding Specialist

Formalities

  1. 1
    Pre-filled from the case. Client classification, booking centre, e-signature eligibility, and correspondence come from earlier steps, each with its source.
  2. 2
    Pre-check results. The generated package is checked against the approved case. Here, one item needs attention.
  3. 3
    The reviewer stays. The specialist signs off the package and the flagged item before anything is sent, which keeps the four-eye control in place.
  4. 4
    One action to send. Sending for signing replaces the 28 manual steps of the current process.
Client, in the bank’s app

Client signing

  1. 1
    Identity already confirmed. The client verified in the app earlier, so signing needs no call or branch visit.
  2. 2
    Explicit consent. The client agrees to sign electronically before the documents unlock.
  3. 3
    One action for every document. The client signs all five documents in one step, whenever it suits the client.

06

Reflection

  1. I would check the 20% figure in week one

    The 20% first-time-right figure carries the business case, and it came from participant recall. Checking it against case management data at the start would have made the strongest argument in the project a proven one.

  2. I look upstream of a bottleneck first

    Due diligence looked like a back-office capacity problem. The research showed it was a data quality problem created upstream, when the advisor wrote the case.

  3. Automation is also a change to people's jobs

    Taking most of the manual work off specialists is the right outcome, but it changes their role. I would bring specialists and HR into the design of that change at the start, while the screens were still sketches.
