Case study, banking compliance

Transparency Register: no case closes without a reason on record

Under a new Swiss law, banks must act on any difference between their own records of a company's beneficial owners and the federal Transparency Register, within fixed deadlines. As product designer and research lead, I designed the case manager that compliance officers use to compare both records, request corrections, and report or close each case with a reason on record.

Role
Product designer, research lead
Engagement
About 6 weeks
Research
6 interviews, 3 shadowing sessions
Status
MVP released

Problem

Since October 2026, a Swiss bank that finds a difference between its records and the Transparency Register must ask the company to correct it, and report any difference that remains within 30 days. Officers compared the two sources by hand and tracked the deadlines in spreadsheets.

What I did

I interviewed six compliance officers, shadowed three working sessions, and mapped the legal process. I then designed a case manager that compares both records field by field, shows the running deadline on every case, and records a reason for every decision.

Outcome

The MVP has been released. Every case shows which fields differ, who holds it, and which deadline runs, and no case closes without a recorded reason.

01

The situation

Since 1 October 2026, Swiss companies must record their beneficial owners, the people who ultimately own or control them, in a federal Transparency Register. Banks may consult the register for their due diligence. When a bank finds that the register differs from its own records, it must first ask the company to correct its entry within a reasonable deadline. If the entry still differs after that deadline, the bank reports the discrepancy to the register within 30 days, and the register adds a note to the company's entry.

At JPB, compliance officers in the first-line know-your-customer (KYC) team work these cases. The client advisor passes requests to the company, and the Business Acceptance Committee (BAC) decides whose record stands when the beneficial owners can't be determined from the file. KYC operations correct the bank's own record.

Before the tool, officers collected the bank's KYC record and the register data from two systems and compared them field by field. They tracked deadlines in spreadsheets or calendars, emailed the client advisor, and decided from memory of the policy documents whether the bank or the register was wrong. They wrote up the reasons for each decision afterwards, which made the record harder to defend in a review.

Constraints

  • The bank's own identification stays decisive. Under the Anti-Money Laundering Act, the register is a source the bank may consult, and it doesn't replace the bank's due diligence.
  • Suspicious activity stays outside the tool. Signs of concealment go to Compliance through the suspicious activity process, and the client and client advisor must not be told.
  • Contact with the client advisor stays outside the tool in this release. The officer records the client's explanation when the answer comes back.
  • One design system. The tool uses the JPB design system it shares with the bank's onboarding platform.

Research and findings

I talked to the officers who would work the queue and watched how they handled a case today. I also worked through the law, because the legal process sets the order of every step and every deadline in a case.

  1. Deadlines drive the risk

    Once the correction deadline has passed, the bank has 30 days to report. Officers tracked both dates outside the case, so the date that carried the legal risk was never in front of them when they opened a case.

  2. Where the error sits decides the next step

    A discrepancy can sit in the bank's record, in the register entry, or in both. KYC operations correct a bank-side error internally. A register-side error needs the company to act, and a report if it doesn't. These rules lived in policy documents, so officers applied them from memory. This finding changed the design from a comparison screen into a case that records where the error is and starts the matching next step.

  3. Differences hide in single fields

    The same owner usually appears in both sources with a different date of birth, address, or share. Comparing those fields by eye was slow, and a change such as 85% to 100% was easy to miss.

  4. The same five situations come up again and again

    Follow-up fell into five recurring scenarios: a bank error, a register error, a red flag, a company that doesn't respond, and an explanation that isn't plausible. Each had a known procedure, but nobody could see it while working a case.

03

Key decisions

1The running deadline on every case, and the queue sorted by it

Every case shows one deadline, which is the correction deadline until it passes and then the report deadline 30 days later. The queue is sorted by that date, cases without a deadline come first, and a warning appears at 7 days left and turns red at 3, whoever holds the case.

Set aside
A separate deadline report, like the spreadsheets officers already kept. It would still sit outside the case, which was the problem.
Cost
The 7-day and 3-day thresholds are a policy choice that Compliance owns, so changing them is a policy change as well as a product one.

2Both records in the same shape, with each difference written out

The bank's record sits above the register entry in two tables with the same columns. Above them, a list names each difference in a sentence, such as “Michael Keller: date of birth differs, 4 Nov 1971 at the bank and 11 Apr 1971 in the register.”

Set aside
The two records side by side. With six columns each, both tables would be too narrow to read on a laptop.
Cost
The list depends on matching each owner across spelling and date variants, and on agreed rules for which field differences count.

3Bank, register, or both as an explicit choice

When the client's explanation comes back, the officer chooses where the discrepancy lies. The selected option states what it starts, whether that's a correction by KYC operations, a correction by the company with both deadlines, or both.

Set aside
A free-text note on where the error is, as officers wrote it before. It records the judgement but starts nothing.
Cost
Choosing the bank has to start a correction process in the KYC system, so the tool depends on that integration.

4The follow-up procedures inside the case

Each case has five tabs, one per recurring scenario, and each tab gives the procedure in three steps. The procedures sit next to the actions they guide.

Set aside
Links to the policy documents. Officers already had those, and decisions still differed from one officer to the next.
Cost
The procedures have to be kept in step with policy, and three steps can't cover every edge case.

5A recorded reason for every escalation and closure

Escalating or closing needs a reason from a fixed list, a written justification, and a confirmation dialog. Closure reasons depend on the case's state. A case can close as reported only once a report is due, and as matching only when no differences remain.

Set aside
Free closure with an optional note. A case could then close as resolved while the comparison still showed differences.
Cost
Every decision takes more steps, and officers write a justification even when the reason seems obvious.

The design

A case moves through seven states, and the queue shows who holds it in each one. The case returns to the officer when the records match, when the correction deadline has passed, or when BAC has decided.

These six screens from the prototype follow a case from the queue to closure. Hover over or select a note to see where it points on the screen.

Escalate to BAC

  1. 1
    Deadlines keep running. Escalating doesn't pause the legal clock, and BAC's decision comes back to this case.
  2. 2
    A reason from a fixed list. Complex ownership structure, Beneficial owners unclear, Explanation not plausible, Client non-responsive, or Other.
  3. 3
    A written justification. It goes to BAC with the discrepancy details and stays in the audit trail.
  4. 4
    A confirmation before the handoff. A dialog restates the reason, names BAC as the next owner, and says the deadlines keep running.
Compliance officer, report due

Close case

  1. 1
    A warning with the facts. Closing is permanent, and the warning says how many discrepancies the comparison still shows.
  2. 2
    Reasons that match the state. Reported to the Transparency Register appears only once a report is due. While differences remain, the other option is a data-entry variant.
  3. 3
    Report details. For a reported case, the officer records the date of the report and the register's reference.
  4. 4
    A second, deliberate step. The red button opens a dialog that restates the reason and says the step can't be undone.

06

Reflection

  1. I would have brought Legal in during the first week

    I built the legal process from published summaries of the new law. Questions such as when the 30 days start, and what counts as a reportable discrepancy, shape the whole case flow. I would have settled them with Legal before drawing any screens.

  2. I would have run the comparison on a sample of clients first

    Nobody knew how many cases the comparison would produce, so I designed for a standing queue without knowing its size. A sample run would also have shown where most errors sit. If most register entries are out of date, the correction step carries most of the load, and that is where I would have spent more design time.

  3. I would have designed the client advisor's side

    The advisor's main worry was getting a compliance task with no context or deadline. In this release, the request reaches the advisor as a message, and the answer comes back outside the tool. A simple advisor view of open requests and their dates would have closed that gap.
